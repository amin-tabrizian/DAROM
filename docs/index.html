Why Does This Matter?

Autonomous vehicles increasingly rely on Roadside Units (RSUs) — smart infrastructure sensors — to perceive the surrounding environment through Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I) communication. This lets a vehicle "see" what its onboard sensors alone cannot, particularly in complex maneuvers like highway on-ramp merging.

However, real-world V2I links are not instantaneous. Under nominal conditions, RSU perception-to-vehicle delivery typically falls within ~100 ms. But edge-computing overload, packet retransmission under congestion, and multi-hop relaying can push latency far higher. A control policy trained on fresh observations will receive stale data at runtime — and acting on outdated information in dense, high-speed traffic can be catastrophic.

Existing deep RL methods for merging simply ignore this latency, while classical MPC approaches struggle to generalize across varying delay distributions. We ask: can we build a single policy that stays safe and effective no matter how the network misbehaves?

The Idea: DAROM

We introduce DAROM (Delay-Aware Reinforcement learning for On-ramp Merging), a framework that turns the latency problem into a tractable learning signal. Three components work together:

  • Delay-Aware Encoder: A GRU-based network that jointly processes the delayed observation, the agent's action history, and the current delay magnitude — reconstructing a latent representation of the true current state.
  • Safety Controller: A physics-based supervisory layer that overrides unsafe actions in real time, eliminating tail-end collision risk even when the policy receives worst-case delayed inputs.
  • Unified RL Agent: A Soft Actor-Critic agent that simultaneously decides longitudinal acceleration and lateral lane-change actions, trained entirely under the uniform delay distribution.

Figure 1. Overall DAROM architecture. The Delay-Aware Encoder (left) processes the delayed observation, action buffer, and delay magnitude to produce a latent state fed into the lane-changing and lane-keeping SAC agents (right).

Figure 2. Augmented state construction. The ego vehicle's onboard state is always current; the RSU observation arrives with delay ωt. Together with the masked action history, these form the full input to the Delay-Aware Encoder.

Results & Experiments

We evaluated all methods over 500 episodes × 3 random seeds in SUMO, using traffic parameters derived from the NGSIM US-101 dataset. Three traffic density levels stress-test the policy: Easy (360 vph/lane), Medium (~700 vph/lane), and Hard (~1400 vph/lane). All delay experiments use Ωmax = 2.0 s.

Figure 3. Training dynamics across Easy, Medium, and Hard modes for GRU, MLP, and Transformer encoders vs. baselines. Rows show average return, critic loss, and actor loss. DAROM-GRU converges consistently across all difficulty levels, while MLP collapses in Hard mode.
