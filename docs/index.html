Motivation

Autonomous vehicles increasingly rely on Roadside Units (RSUs) — smart infrastructure sensors — to perceive the surrounding environment through Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I) communication. This lets a vehicle "see" what its onboard sensors alone cannot, particularly in complex maneuvers like highway on-ramp merging.

However, real-world V2I links are not instantaneous. Under nominal conditions, RSU perception-to-vehicle delivery typically falls within ~100 ms. But edge-computing overload, packet retransmission under congestion, and multi-hop relaying can push latency far higher. A control policy trained on fresh observations will receive stale data at runtime — and acting on outdated information in dense, high-speed traffic can be catastrophic.

Existing deep RL methods for merging simply ignore this latency, while classical MPC approaches struggle to generalize across varying delay distributions. We ask: can we build a single policy that stays safe and effective no matter how the network misbehaves?

The Idea: DAROM

We introduce DAROM (Delay-Aware Reinforcement learning for On-ramp Merging), a framework that recasts the latency problem as a Random Delay Markov Decision Process (RMDMP). Instead of receiving the current environment state, the agent observes a delayed version ot alongside its own action history ut and the received delay magnitude ωt. Together these form the augmented state xt = (ot, ut, ωt), which recovers the Markov property despite stochastic latency.

Three components work together to solve the RMDMP:

  • Delay-Aware Encoder: Processes the augmented state through a GRU-based architecture. The GRU captures temporal dependencies in the action history — allowing the encoder to infer what the true current traffic state likely is, even when the received observation is seconds old.
  • Physics-Based Safety Controller: A supervisory layer that overrides unsafe actions using kinematic constraints derived from the ego vehicle's onboard sensors, which are always delay-free. This eliminates tail-end collision risk regardless of how stale the RSU observation is.
  • Unified SAC Agent: A Soft Actor-Critic agent trained on the latent state produced by the encoder. It simultaneously outputs a continuous longitudinal acceleration and a discrete lateral lane-change decision, trained entirely under the uniform delay distribution yet generalizing across all tested delay profiles.

Results & Experiments

All simulations are performed in SUMO, with traffic demand and surrounding vehicle speed profiles extracted from the NGSIM US Highway 101 dataset to reflect real-world highway conditions. Surrounding vehicles are modeled with two driver profiles — aggressive (minimum headway 0.1–0.7 s) and cooperative (0.6–0.8 s) — sampled per episode to capture heterogeneous traffic behavior. The RSU delay is modeled as ωt ∈ [0, Ωmax], with Ωmax = 20 time-steps (2.0 s at Δt = 0.1 s), representing a deliberate worst-case stress test covering edge-computing overload, packet retransmission, and multi-hop relaying scenarios.

We compare three DAROM encoder variants (GRU, MLP, Transformer) against a No-Encoder ablation, a receding-horizon MPC baseline with delay compensation, and DRL-ORMOC, a state-of-the-art deep RL method for on-ramp merging that does not model delays. All DRL agents are trained for 7,000,000 steps with the SAC algorithm (learning rate 3×10−5, batch size 512) and evaluated over 500 episodes × 3 random seeds across three traffic density modes.

Figure 3. Training dynamics across Easy, Medium, and Hard modes.

DAROM-GRU converges steadily across all difficulty modes with decreasing critic and actor losses, while the MLP encoder collapses entirely in Hard mode and weaker baselines show noisier, less consistent learning curves.

DAROM-GRU achieves 99.80% success with 0.00% collisions in Hard mode, outperforming MPC (83.10%) and DRL-ORMOC (90.09%). Explicit delay modeling is critical — without it, safety cannot be guaranteed even at comparable success rates.
